Overview

Buying for a room, not a cart.

A tile changes the quantity. A fixture changes the installation. A late delivery changes the schedule. Takeoff connects those decisions inside an assistant conversation, with a record that survives the chat.

Project context

Connect materials to the room, the schedule, and the people responsible.

Clear decisions

Make quantities, approvals, and unknowns visible before anyone pays.

Honest limits

Fourteen simulated interactions. Participant testing and live integrations remain next steps.

Solution

One Spec. Shared context.

A Spec keeps the room, selected product, source, quantity, approvals, and order state together. The owner, trade, and supplier see one decision with different responsibilities—not three disconnected versions.

Inspect before you commit.

Change the layout, inspect the quantity, then separate technical confirmation from spending approval.

Core Flows

Start with what you know.

Attach the room, ask the next useful question, review a sample, and capture an installer’s words with their source.

Research

Three people. One purchase.

One short film brings the roles, needs, risks, and pilot questions together. Desk research shaped the concept; it does not establish adoption or measured benefit.

Research notes & film transcript

Three roles

The homeowner chooses and approves spend. The contractor checks fit, quantity, and installation. The supplier confirms stock, fulfilment, and delivery. A shared record needs to serve all three.

Five needs

  • Make choices before dependent work starts.
  • Check quantities and existing orders separately.
  • Compare delivery windows with the site schedule.
  • Record who approved what.
  • Keep samples and exact specifications together.

Four risks

  • Trust: homeowners may want assistance without autonomous buying. Keep release under their control.
  • Workflow: suppliers may prefer calls and portals. Work with their systems and keep a human route.
  • Delivery: an estimate is not a promise. Separate estimated and confirmed dates.
  • Duplicate orders: quantity math is not order history. Check other channels before release.

Adoption tests

Ask homeowners when they would approve in chat. Check whether contractors lose a spreadsheet or gain another record. Ask suppliers whether the handoff reduces missing information. None of these outcomes is validated yet.

Pilot materials

Tiles: layout, waste, batch match. Flooring: laying direction, packs, installation. Paint: color, sheen, preparation, coverage. Lighting: mounting, output, dimmer compatibility, lead time.

Next test

Start with one category and one supplier. Observe a real owner–installer–supplier handoff. Test fewer repeated checks and ordering errors rather than promise savings. Add confirmed delivery data before claiming live tracking.

Before You Order

Check what could stop the job.

A promising recommendation is not an order-ready specification. Check dimensions, timing, missing information, and access before asking anyone to commit.

Handoff & Follow-through

The decision travels with the order.

Carry the evidence to the installer. Keep delivery photos and post-order issues attached to the same project record.

Test the handoff, not the pitch.

Ask an owner and installer to resolve a late fixture and an unknown specification. Then ask a supplier to use the resulting record. These studies are ready for critique, not reported as user-tested.

Recall

Can the owner recover the choice and its reason without replaying the thread?

Timing

Does the prompt surface an uncertainty before it becomes a costly commitment?

Handoff

Can the trade and supplier use the record outside the owner’s assistant?

Design Decisions

Keep the reason. Show the unknown.

A new message must not silently replace an approved specification. Keep the source of a decision attached, and route missing information to someone who can verify it.

Proposed state model: suggested, needs confirmation, approved, ordered, received. A change to the material, quantity, price, or date can require renewed approval.

Real-world Constraints

Confirmation is not a guess.

A photo does not certify a dimension. An arrival window is not a delivery promise. The interface must show what is known, who checked it, and what still needs an answer.

Authority

The owner approves spend. The trade checks fit. The supplier confirms fulfilment.

Release

Stop when required technical information, approval, or supplier confirmation is missing.

Changes

Show the new version, preserve the old decision, and request approval again where needed.

Reflection

Less autonomy. More clarity.

Connections

Architectural thinking makes the relationships between room, material, schedule, and people visible.

Controls

Chat carries context. People still need clear ways to inspect, compare, approve, and hand off.

Evidence

The next step is a real purchasing handoff—not another claim about what the concept can do.
