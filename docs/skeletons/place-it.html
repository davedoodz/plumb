Problem

Furniture comes back because it didn’t fit, not because they stopped liking it

A product photo tells you what a lamp looks like. It can’t tell you whether it clears the table, blocks the sightline across it, or gets through the front door. So buyers guess, and the ones who guess wrong send it back.

What the numbers say

Furniture bought online comes back four times as often as furniture bought in a store

In-store furniture returns sit around 5%. Online, the same category runs 18–25%, and one large return costs the merchant $55–108 to process before restocking. 71% of US consumers now buy furniture online anyway.

More than half of those returns are about size and space

Furniture-specific return analysis puts size and space mismatch at 58% — split between “larger than expected” (34%) and “doesn’t fit the space” (24%). Colour and material gaps and transit damage follow, from separate broad-retail studies, so the bars overlap and don’t sum to 100.

Sources: NRF / Happy Returns 2025 returns landscape; RocketReturns 2025 category analysis; YouGov 2025; Eightx and First Chair benchmark roundups (2026). Vendor figures are labelled as such on the page.

What already exists

Putting the object in the room is solved. Deciding isn’t.

Commerce platforms have shipped native 3D and AR on product pages for years — upload a GLB and USDZ and “view in your space” appears on iOS and Android with no app. IKEA Place did true-scale placement in 2017; IKEA Kreativ now scans a whole room at ~97% accuracy and toggles product dimensions and spacing. Vendors claim 25–40% fewer returns for shoppers who use AR. None of them tell you whether the pendant is hung right.

Where the rules actually live

The decision is buried in blog posts, and the blog posts disagree

How high should a pendant hang above a table? Lighting retailers say 28–34 in, 30–36 in, 36–42 in for taller ceilings, or 36–44 in — measured from the tabletop, except entryway pendants, which are measured from the floor. Add the inset rule (fixture at least 12 in narrower than the table), the 7 ft walkway clearance, and the cord-length arithmetic, and the buyer is doing an architect’s job with a tape measure and four browser tabs.

That’s the gap: the AR tools render the object, the guides hold the rules, and nothing puts the rule on the object in the room.

What this means for the design

Don’t compete on AR

Placement at true scale is a solved, free layer. Use Quick Look. Spend the effort on what happens after the object lands.

Put the rule on the object

Three checks — drop, inset, door — drawn where the buyer is looking, in the buyer’s units, with a pass or a fix. Not a dimensions panel.

Sell the cord length, not the lamp

Ceiling height minus table height minus clearance is a number the shop can pre-fill. That’s the difference between a return and a repeat customer.

Two things the research changed. The door check moved from a nice-to-have to a first-class flow, because “doesn’t fit the space” is a quarter of all furniture returns and no competitor touches it. And “larger than expected” at 34% means scale perception is the single biggest lever — so the object has to land at true scale before anything else is drawn, or the annotations inherit the wrong number.
